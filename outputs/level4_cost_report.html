How to read it. Poor-health person-years = the whole population × the years each person is expected to live in poor health (LE − HLY). It rises with ageing but falls as healthy-life years improve, so the net is near-flat. The East (younger, shorter lives) carries a smaller burden than its population alone implies; the West's larger elderly cohorts dominate.

Does the burden drive the sector's cost? (descriptive — no causal claim)

We relate the burden to NACE-Q value added (human health & social work). Across countries the two scale together — but that is a size artefact (big countries have both). Within a country over time, the link vanishes.

Cross-country, 2024 (log–log)
Strong cross-sectional fit (FE elasticity 0.1, R² 0.991) — but this is mostly country size: both burden and sector scale with population.
Within-country, year-on-year
First-differenced elasticity 0.013, R² 0.001, correlation 0.026 (149 obs).

In plain terms: changes in the poor-health burden explain essentially none of the year-to-year change in the sector's value added. NACE-Q grows ~2.4%/yr with wages, prices and the wider economy, while the burden moves ~1.06%/yr — the two are statistically unrelated in the short run. So we forecast the sector on its own trend, not from the demographic burden.
Honest limitations. • LE − HLY is a mechanical identity, and HLY is a self-perceived (GALI) survey measure — the burden inherits that subjectivity.
• The cost link is descriptive, not causal; the within-country result says the demographic burden is not a usable short-run predictor of the NACE-Q sector (which tracks GDP).
• Bracket measures (government health gov_10a_exp GF07 + social GF10, and the SHA health accounts) tell the same story — sector cost is an economic, not a purely demographic, quantity.
