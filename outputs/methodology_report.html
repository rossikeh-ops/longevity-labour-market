Methodology — how the model works

A transparent ensemble of simple statistical models + Monte-Carlo — deliberately no neural networks (short series, defensibility required). Three diagrams describe the core model (Levels 0–3); Levels 4–6 reuse the same engine for the health, retirement and macroeconomic extensions.

📋 Data sources behind the model ↓

Method in one paragraph

Each driver series is forecast on its full history by an ensemble of simple models (linear trend, damped Holt, drift, naive, AR(1)) — damping and the naive anchor stop saturating rates from over-extrapolating, and the member weights adapt by forecast horizon. Population uses Eurostat's projection proj_23np, calibrated to observed 2024. Supply = healthy working-age people × expected working life; demand = (employed + vacancies) × required service. Monte-Carlo bands combine model disagreement, out-of-sample backtest error and demographic uncertainty.

The pipeline — Levels 0 → 6

Where each step sits and what it produces. Levels 1–2 run every driver through the ensemble engine (§1), then combine via the identities below; Level 3 subtracts the two. Levels 4–6 (open horizons) reuse the same engine for descriptive health, retirement and macroeconomic extensions.

1Ensemble forecast engine

For each time series (for every country × sex, over its full history) we do not rely on a single model — we average 5 simple models: linear trend, damped Holt, drift, naive (last value held flat) and AR(1) mean-reversion. In the code that is exactly one line:

src/forecast.pyDEFAULT_MEMBERS = ("linear", "holt", "drift", "naive", "ar1")

Damped Holt and the naive anchor guard against over-extrapolation (socio-economic rates saturate, they do not grow linearly forever), while AR(1) pulls cyclical series back toward their long-run mean — and the member weights adapt by forecast horizon (see below).

👉 Try it live: the interactive ensemble uncertainty visualizer — drag the ensemble size and sampling noise and watch a cloud of simple models (and their average) form the uncertainty band, exactly as described here. No neural nets, just simple models + Monte-Carlo.

The uncertainty band (the amber area in the reports) is assembled from three sources, combined in quadrature: (1) the disagreement between the models; (2) the real out-of-sample error from backtesting — we re-forecast history and measure how far off we are; and (3) the demographic uncertainty of the population. That is why the bands are a realistic 12–43% wide, not a falsely narrow ±5%.

On the supply side, the engine forecasts life expectancy, the healthy-life share (HLY/LE), working-life duration and the working-age population to 2033, which are then combined into healthy person-years.

Supply = healthy working-age people × expected working life person-years

1bWouldn't separate 3/6/9-year models be more accurate? We tested it — no.

A natural question: surely a model dedicated to the 9-year forecast would beat one general model? We checked it directly. In a rolling-origin backtest over the smooth supply drivers, we compared our single horizon-aware ensemble against separate "direct" models — one fitted specifically for each horizon (an OLS of the value h years ahead on today's value). Among the models that actually extrapolate, the ensemble wins at every horizon and the gap widens the further out you go. But the honest caveat is the third line: the naïve floor (last value held flat) is the lowest of all three on these near-random-walk drivers — so the real win is horizon-aware restraint that hugs that floor, not a trend-hungry specialist that drifts off it.

Forecast error by horizon — ensemble vs separate models
horizon-aware ensemble separate per-horizon models naïve floor — lowest of the three. At 9 years out: naïve 5.0% < ensemble 5.4% < direct 5.8%.
…because a dedicated long-horizon model starves
Only 3 training pairs. A separate 9-year model can learn from just 3 (year, year+9) examples — far too few, so it overfits and does worse exactly where you hoped it would help.

The reason is data, not cleverness: every extra year of horizon throws away another year of usable training pairs (15 → 3), so a long-horizon specialist is estimated from almost nothing and overfits. The single ensemble pools the full history and stays close to the naive floor (the best achievable on these near-random-walk drivers). One horizon-aware ensemble is the more accurate and more honest choice. (See the Model trust report for the full error-by-horizon.)

See it for yourself. The model-flexibility playground lets you drag one slider from underfit to overfit on a real Healthy-Life-Years series, with the cross-validated optimum — and the classic train-error-falls / CV-error-U-shape tradeoff — computed live. It is the same lesson, made tactile: on 13–21 years of data, the sweet spot is a modest flexibility, and a neural network would sit far into the overfit zone.

2Building labour demand

From the forecast components we build demand using the formula from the case brief. Population is not extrapolated naively — we take Eurostat's official projection proj_23np, calibrated to the observed 2024 value.

3Building labour supply

The same logic as demand, but with two health–demographic multipliers. Both multipliers — the healthy-years share (HLY ÷ LE) and expected working life — are forecast with the same 5-model ensemble from the first diagram. The result is in person-years, exactly like demand — which is why the two are directly comparable.

Together — the whole model

  1. Ensemble forecast engine — how a single series is forecast.
  2. Demand = jobs × required length of service.
  3. Supply = healthy working-age people × expected working life.

Level 3 simply subtracts: Balance = Supply − Demand (in human-working-years) — the headline result, visualised in the balance report. Levels 4–6 then reuse the very same engine for three descriptive extensions — the poor-health burden, the healthy-retirement dividend and the macroeconomic labour-vs-productivity split — detailed below.

Levels 4–6 — open horizons (the same engine, reused)

The three extension levels forecast different health–demographic quantities with the same 5-model ensemble, then test — honestly and descriptively — whether each drives an economic outcome. These are relationships and an identity decomposition, not causal predictors: where the link turns out to be ≈ 0, we say so plainly rather than pretend to forecast it.

4Cost of unhealthy years

The poor-health burden is population × the years lived in poor health (life expectancy minus healthy-life years), by sex, summed and forecast to 2033. We then relate it to the NACE-Q health & social-work sector's value added: across countries it co-moves (mostly country size), but within a country over time the elasticity is ≈ 0 — the sector tracks GDP, not the demographic burden.

Burden = Population × (LE − HLY)person-years lived in poor health

5Healthy retirement dividend

Healthy years lived beyond the statutory retirement age (floored at zero), population-scaled, by sex, forecast to 2033. Tested against leisure / education / culture consumption (COICOP CP09–11): cross-country it co-moves, but within-country the link is ≈ 0 — consumption tracks household income, not the dividend.

Dividend = Population × max(0, HLY − retirement age)healthy person-years after retirement

6Longevity in the macroeconomy

Real GDP is decomposed by the identity GDP = employment × productivity; growth accounting splits each country's projected growth into a labour channel (where longevity acts) and a productivity channel. The reverse arrow — does healthy longevity lift productivity? — is absent: cross-country the correlation has the wrong sign (a self-perceived-health / GALI artifact) and within a country it is ≈ 0.

Real GDP = Employment × Productivitygrowth = labour + productivity
