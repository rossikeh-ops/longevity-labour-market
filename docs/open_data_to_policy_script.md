# Speaker script — *From Open Data to Evidence-Based Policy*
### A digital dashboard for monitoring healthy longevity and labour markets

A first-person spoken script matching the 17-slide deck
(`open_data_to_policy_healthy_longevity.pptx`). **Total ≈ 18–20 minutes** at a
natural pace (~135 words/min); trim the *italic asides* to land nearer 15.
Each section = one slide, with a **stage cue** and a **budget**.

> Tip: replace **[your name]** on slides 1 and 17 before you present.

---

## Slide 1 · Title  *(0:30)*
*On screen: title slide.*

Good morning, and thank you for having me. My name is **[your name]**, and today
I want to talk about something that sits right at the meeting point of my
professional past and my academic present: how we turn **open data into evidence
for policy** — and specifically, a digital dashboard for monitoring **healthy
longevity** and the **labour market**. Let me start with how I got here.

---

## Slide 2 · About me — the journey  *(1:30)*
*On screen: "From treating disease to extending healthspan".*

For eight years I worked in the private healthcare sector, as a **marketing
manager in the medical-device industry** — my focus was **CPAP devices and hearing
aids**. That may sound niche, but it put me very close to the everyday reality of
**chronic and age-related conditions** — sleep apnoea, hearing loss — the kind of
conditions that quietly erode a person's ability to function, to work, to stay
engaged. And it taught me something I keep coming back to: that medical
technology, used well, doesn't just treat disease — it **preserves function and
quality of life**.

Over those years my thinking gradually shifted. I became less interested in
treating disease *after* it appears, and more interested in the bigger question
underneath it: how do we keep people **healthy and functional for longer**? How do
we extend not just lifespan, but **healthspan** — the years lived in good health?
*That* question is what moved me from the commercial world into research.

---

## Slide 3 · About me — research focus  *(1:15)*
*On screen: the four dimensions (scientific / economic / technological / societal).*

Today I'm a **PhD researcher at the University of National and World Economy**,
where I study the development of longevity science and the evolution of theories
of ageing. What draws me in is that longevity is genuinely **interdisciplinary** —
it is scientific, economic, technological and societal, all at once. I'm
interested in how the science of ageing can be **translated into real value**:
into healthcare innovation, sustainable business models, public policy, and
broader benefits for society. *My aim, in one sentence, is to help build an
interdisciplinary understanding of healthy longevity — bringing together what I
learned in private-sector healthcare with what I study in research.*

---

## Slide 4 · The challenge  *(1:15)*
*On screen: "Ageing is not only a health question…".*

So here is the challenge as I see it. We usually talk about ageing as a **health**
problem. But ageing is also — perhaps primarily — an **economic and labour-market**
problem. As populations grow older, and across much of Europe's East also
**smaller**, the pressing questions become very concrete: who stays healthy enough
to work? For how long? And can each economy still supply the labour its jobs
actually need? To answer those questions we need **evidence** — open, comparable,
transparent — that links healthy longevity to the labour market and turns it into
something policy can act on. And that is exactly what a dashboard can do.

---

## Slide 5 · The approach  *(1:15)*
*On screen: the open-data → dashboard → policy pipeline.*

The approach is a simple pipeline. It begins with **open data** — public Eurostat
series on population, life expectancy and healthy life years, working life,
employment and vacancies. Then a **transparent engine** — deliberately simple,
explainable models, no black boxes — converts those averages into a single common
currency. That feeds a **digital dashboard** that compares eight countries, by
sex, all the way to 2033. And the whole point is the last step: **evidence for
policy** — quantified levers and scenarios that decision-makers can interrogate
and trust. Open data in; actionable evidence out.

---

## Slide 6 · The dashboard  *(1:15)*
*On screen: "healthy longevity, in labour-market terms".*

Let me show you the tool itself. The core idea is that you cannot weigh a
life-expectancy number against a job vacancy — different units. So the dashboard
converts demographic averages into **human-working-years**, a stock that *is*
directly comparable with jobs. **Supply** is healthy working-age people times
their expected working life. **Demand** is the jobs the economy runs — employed
plus vacancies — times the required length of service. **Balance** is simply the
difference. And on top of that core it monitors the poor-health burden, the
healthy-retirement dividend, and the macro-economy — all from the same open data,
for eight countries, from 2011 out to 2033.

---

## Slide 7 · The landscape  *(1:15)*
*On screen: existing dashboards + the gap.*

Now, I want to be honest about where this sits, because we are certainly not the
first to look at ageing and labour. There is a whole landscape of dashboards — the
**OECD's Dashboard on Older Workers**, **Eurostat's Ageing Europe** tools, the ILO's
**ILOSTAT** explorer, the **UNECE Active Ageing Index**, and national ones in the UK,
Canada and the US. They are valuable. But if you look at what they actually track,
it is remarkably consistent: employment at 55-to-64 and 65-plus, participation by
age band, effective versus statutory retirement, dependency ratios, pension
sustainability. In other words, they monitor **chronological age and headcount**.
What they largely *don't* capture is **functional capacity — healthspan — and the
longevity dividend**. And that gap is exactly where the interesting questions live.

---

## Slide 8 · The paradigm shift  *(1:30)*
*On screen: the traditional vs healthy-longevity comparison table.*

This is the heart of it: a **paradigm shift, from chronological age to functional
capacity**. A traditional ageing-and-labour dashboard uses chronological age —
those arbitrary cut-offs, fifty-plus, sixty-five-plus. Its problem statement is
falling supply and rising pension pressure. It measures exit by statutory versus
effective retirement age; it sees the workforce as a **static cohort at the end of
a career funnel**; and its interventions are things like raising the retirement age.

A **healthy-longevity** dashboard reframes all of that. The lens becomes
**biological and functional capacity** — healthspan versus lifespan. The problem is
no longer just declining supply; it is the **mismatch between people's capacity and
how our workplaces are designed**. Exit is measured against **healthy,
disability-free years**, not just a legal age. The workforce becomes a **dynamic
life-course** — where people reskill and adapt across a career that may span sixty
years. And the interventions change accordingly: prevention, ergonomic adaptation,
phased retirement, career pivots. Our dashboard already takes that healthspan lens
— because it measures **healthy life years, not just headcount**. Let me show you
what that reveals.

---

## Slide 9 · Evidence 1 — the longevity dividend  *(1:15)*
*On screen: "already holding the line".*

The first finding is genuinely hopeful. Across these eight countries, working-age
populations are **shrinking** — and yet labour supply stays **almost flat** out to
2033. How? Because people are **healthier, work longer, and participate more**.
This is the **longevity dividend**, and we can measure it directly — through
healthy life years as a share of life expectancy. The insight I want to leave you
with is this: healthy longevity is not only a medical outcome. It is a
**quantifiable economic resource**. Extend people's healthspan, and you literally
**expand the workforce**.

---

## Slide 10 · Evidence 2 — the balance  *(1:15)*
*On screen: East surplus vs West shortage.*

The second finding is about *where* that balance holds, and where it breaks. In
aggregate, across all eight countries, supply and demand come out **roughly
balanced** — a net of about **plus 89 million** human-working-years. But that
average is a mirage. Split it East versus West and you see two opposite realities:
the **East** — Poland, Romania, Czechia, Bulgaria — runs a surplus of about **plus
370 million**. The **West and EFTA** — Germany, France, Switzerland, Norway — run a
shortage of about **minus 280 million**. Two opposite imbalances that cancel on
paper — and that, in a single number, is the demographic engine behind West-bound
migration. *Because the balance is a small difference of two large forecasts, the
dashboard shows it as a direction with honest uncertainty bands — validated by
backtesting, with a supply error of about three-and-a-half percent.*

---

## Slide 11 · Evidence 3 — health & the economy  *(1:00)*
*On screen: burden / dividend / macro layers.*

The dashboard doesn't stop at the labour balance. It also monitors the
**poor-health burden** — the person-years people live in poor health, the pressure
on health and long-term care. It monitors the **healthy-retirement dividend** — the
healthy years people have beyond retirement age, which is the very base of the
active-retiree, or "silver", economy. And it links to the **macro-economy**, where
longevity acts through the labour channel. And we are honest here: within a
country, over time, these causal links come out close to **zero** — spending tracks
income, not demography. So we describe patterns; we never over-claim causation.

---

## Slide 12 · The frontier  *(1:15)*
*On screen: the healthy-longevity data layer.*

So where does this go next? This is the **frontier**, and it is where my research
points. A true healthy-longevity dashboard has to go **beyond standard labour
statistics**. I see four layers to add. First, the **workability gap**: healthy
life years at fifty and sixty-five, disability-free life expectancy against the age
people actually exit, and the **Work Ability Index**. Second, **workplace
adaptation**: ergonomics, phased and flexible pathways, mid-to-late-career
well-being. Third, **lifelong human capital**: mid-career reskilling and digital
fluency for workers in their fifties, and genuinely age-diverse workplaces. And
fourth, **unpaid contribution**: the caregiving that quietly cannibalises paid
work, and the civic and mentorship roles healthy seniors take on. *This needs a
data shift too — from labour-force surveys toward longitudinal ageing studies like
SHARE, ELSA and HRS, the WHO Global Health Observatory, and occupational-health data.*

---

## Slide 13 · My dissertation  *(1:30)*
*On screen: "The mutual effect of longevity and business".*

This brings me to my own research. My dissertation is on **the mutual effect of
longevity and business** — and I want to stress that word, *mutual*, because it
runs in **both directions**. In one direction, **longevity shapes business**:
longer, healthier lives create entirely new markets and reshape the workforce — the
silver economy, longevity technologies, prevention-oriented healthcare, and a
labour force that is older but also healthier. In the other direction, **business
shapes longevity**: healthcare innovation and medical technology — and here I come
right back to where I began, to CPAP devices and hearing aids — **preserve function
and extend healthspan**, which feeds directly back into how long, and how well,
people can work. So it is a **loop**: business extends healthspan; healthspan
enables a longer working life; and a longer, healthier working life sustains the
economy that funds the next wave of innovation. My question is how to understand —
and how to *measure* — that loop.

To give it a concrete shape: the **silver economy** — the products, services and
business models built around older, healthier consumers — is already one of
Europe's fastest-growing markets, and medical-device innovation sits right at its
core. So understanding this mutual effect is not only academic: it tells businesses
where the **durable demand** will be, and it tells policymakers which innovations
are genuinely worth backing.

---

## Slide 14 · The labour market is the bridge  *(1:15)*
*On screen: longevity → supply, business → demand, balance = the market.*

And this is the connection I most want you to take away: **the labour market is
where longevity and business meet.** It is the bridge. **Longevity** shapes the
**supply** side — healthy people times working life; who can work, and for how long.
**Business and the wider economy** shape the **demand** side — jobs times required
service; the labour the economy actually runs. And their **balance** simply *is* the
labour market — surplus, shortage, and the pressure to migrate. So the "mutual
effect of longevity and business" is not an abstraction. It is precisely the
**labour-market balance** — and the dashboard makes it measurable, in one currency,
out to 2033.

---

## Slide 15 · From data to policy  *(1:15)*
*On screen: three levers + the healthspan "master switch".*

What does this mean for policy? The dashboard shows that only **three families of
levers** can move the balance. You can **increase supply** — participation,
retention of healthy older workers, migration. You can **ease demand** — automation
and productivity, so each job needs fewer human hours. Or you can **maintain
balance** — education, training, retirement-age policy. But there is one lever that
behaves differently from all the others — I'd call it the **master switch**, and it
is **healthspan**. When you raise healthy life years, you do what no single policy
can do alone: you **expand the workforce, shrink the care burden, and unlock the
active-retirement economy — all at once**. And that is really the thesis of this
whole talk: **prevention is not only health policy — prevention is labour-market
policy.**

---

## Slide 16 · Conclusion  *(1:00)*
*On screen: "measurable, comparable, and actionable".*

So, to conclude. Healthy longevity is **measurable, comparable, and actionable**.
Open data and a transparent dashboard turn ageing from an abstract anxiety into
**evidence that policy can actually use** — and they reveal that the mutual effect
of longevity and business runs **straight through the labour market**. My own aim,
drawing on both my private-sector experience and my research, is to help move
healthcare from a model built around **treating disease** toward one built around
**prevention and extending healthspan**. Because if we get that shift right, longer
lives don't have to be a burden — they can be a **dividend**.

---

*On screen: thank-you slide.*

Thank you very much. I'd be glad to take your questions and to discuss any of this
further — and if you'd like to explore the dashboard yourself, it is public and
online. Thank you.
