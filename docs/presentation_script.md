# Presentation script — *Longevity & the Labour Market*

A spoken, story-style narration for **two presenters**, matching the site's **Presentation track** (the numbered 1–10 strip on the homepage). Total run-time **~28½ minutes** of talking, leaving ~1½ min for the handoff and a question — **under 30 minutes**.

- **Presenter A — "The answer"** — stops 1–6 (~15 min): the question, the findings, what they mean.
- **Presenter B — "Why believe it"** — stops 7–10 (~13½ min): how it works, and how far to trust it.

**Pacing:** written for ~120 spoken words/min, which leaves room for pauses, pointing at charts, and the live demos. Each stop shows its **budget** and the **running total**. Read conversationally — these are *talking* lines. Each stop opens with a **stage cue** (what to click); trim the *italic asides* first if you're running long, expand the demos if you're running short.

# 🟢 PRESENTER A — "The answer"  *(stops 1–6 · ~15 min)*

## 1 · Intro + KPIs — the question  *(3 min · total 3:00)*
*On screen: homepage, then click **1 · Intro + KPIs**.*

Good morning, and thank you for being here. We're going to split this between the two of us: I'll walk you through **what we found**, and then my colleague will show you **why you can trust it**.

Here's the backdrop. Europe is getting older — and across much of the East, it's also getting smaller. That's not a distant problem: age-related public spending in the EU is already around a quarter of GDP, and the ratio of over-sixty-fives to working-age people is on track to climb from about 36 percent today toward 55 percent by mid-century. So the question we set out to answer is simple to say and hard to do: **by 2033, can each economy still supply the labour its jobs actually need?**

The hard part is units. You can't weigh a life-expectancy number against a job vacancy — they don't share a scale. So we built a translator. We convert demographic averages into **one common currency — human-working-years** — for eight countries: Bulgaria, Poland, Czechia, Romania, Germany, France, Norway and Switzerland, split by sex, all the way to 2033. The idea is one clean equation: **supply** is healthy working-age people times how long they actually work; **demand** is the jobs the economy runs — employees plus vacancies — times the length of service those careers require; and **balance** is just the difference.

If you scroll down the introduction, you can see exactly how we get here — **seven steps**, from raw Eurostat data, to one tidy harmonised panel, to forecasts, all reproducible and deliberately simple. No neural networks, no black boxes.

And here's the headline, sitting right there in the KPI board. In aggregate, Europe is **roughly balanced** — a net of about **plus 89 million** human-working-years. But that average hides everything. Underneath it is a sharp **East–West divide**: the East runs a surplus of about **+370 million**; the West and EFTA run a shortage of about **−280 million**. *That single fact — surplus East, shortage West — is the demographic engine behind much of Europe's migration.* Let me show you how that number is built, starting with supply.

---

## 2 · Level 1 · Supply — who can work  *(2.5 min · total 5:30)*
*On screen: click **2 · Supply**; show the map.*

Level one: labour **supply** — who can actually work. We take healthy, working-age people and multiply by their expected working life. That gives us supply in human-working-years.

Here's the surprise, and it's the most hopeful number in the whole study. Working-age populations are **shrinking** — especially in the East — and yet total supply **barely moves**: from about **4,490 down to 4,390 million** by 2033, only about **minus two and a half percent**. How can the headcount fall while the labour barely does? Because three forces push back. People are **healthier** for longer; they **work more years**; and **participation is rising**, especially among women and older workers. Together that's the **longevity dividend** — and it's not a future hope, it's already holding the line today.

But the *average* hides the geography. The split is starkly demographic: **Romania and Bulgaria fall hardest** — emigration stacked on top of ageing — while **Germany, France and Norway actually gain** a few percent. The map shows that gradient at a glance: the East draining, the West holding or growing.

*One caveat we keep right at the front: supply here is a **potential ceiling**.* It's net of health and working life, but it's *before* skills mismatch and labour-market frictions. So read it as the maximum the demography allows — not a promise that every one of those years gets worked. And, as always, every number you see carries an uncertainty band.

---

## 3 · Level 2 · Demand — what the economy needs  *(2.5 min · total 8:00)*
*On screen: click **3 · Demand**.*

Level two: **demand** — the labour the economy actually runs. We take the jobs — people employed plus the open vacancies — and multiply by the length of service those careers require. That's demand, in the same human-working-years currency, so it's directly comparable to supply.

And demand is also **broadly flat**: from about **4,420 down to 4,310 million**, again roughly minus two and a half percent. That flatness is the key insight of this level. **Demand doesn't run away.** The imbalance we're going to see is *not* an explosion in how much labour the economy needs — it's almost entirely about **where the working-age people are**. Geography, not appetite.

Now, one honest piece of engineering. **Vacancies are our weakest input** — they're small, shock-driven, and behave almost like a random walk, so simply extrapolating them would be noisy and misleading. Instead we tie them to unemployment through a **Beveridge curve** — the well-established relationship that vacancies fall when unemployment rises. That keeps the vacancy forecast **stable and scenario-able** without pretending we can predict it precisely. *We even validated which occupied-jobs data source to anchor it on — but my colleague will get into exactly how trustworthy all of this is.* The takeaway for now: demand is steady, so the whole story lives on the supply side and on the map.

---

## 4 · Level 3 · Balance — the climax  *(3 min · total 11:00)*
*On screen: click **4 · Balance**; show the balance map.*

Now the headline of the whole project. **Balance is supply minus demand** — and because it's a small difference between two very large numbers, this is where everything sharpens into focus.

At the European level, supply and demand almost perfectly cancel: a **net of about plus 89 million** human-working-years across all eight countries. Taken alone, that sounds reassuring — "Europe is basically fine." But that aggregate is a **mirage**. Split it East versus West and the real structure appears.

The **East runs a surplus of about +370 million** — Poland, Romania, Czechia and Bulgaria have *more* potential labour than their own economies demand. The **West and EFTA run a shortage of about −280 million** — Germany, France, Switzerland and Norway need *more* than their demography can supply. So the continent isn't balanced; it's **two opposite imbalances that happen to cancel on paper**. And that structural truth — surplus in the East, shortage in the West — is precisely the demographic basis for decades of West-bound migration within Europe.

*Now the honesty, because it matters most here.* Balance is a small difference of two big forecasts, so its *relative* error is amplified — on the order of twenty-odd percent. So we deliberately **do not** read it as a precise point. We read it as a **direction with wide, honest bands**: a structural East-surplus, West-shortage signal. And it's a direction we have **stress-tested hard** — including holding out the COVID years and predicting straight through them — which my colleague will show you in detail. If you remember one thing from my half, make it this map.

---

## 5 · Open horizons · Levels 4–6  *(2 min · total 13:00)*
*On screen: click **5 · Open horizons** (Levels 4–6).*

Beyond the core balance, we pushed into three "open horizons" — and here the most important thing is what we **honestly did not find**.

Level four measures the **poor-health burden** — the person-years people live in poor health, which comes out around four-and-a-quarter *billion* — and asks whether it drives **health spending**. Level five measures the **healthy-retirement dividend** — the healthy years left after the retirement age — and asks whether it lifts spending on **leisure, education and culture**. Level six decomposes the **macroeconomy** — GDP as employment times productivity, growing from about eight to eight-and-a-half trillion euros, a bit under one percent a year, almost all of it productivity, not extra workers.

The descriptive headline: across countries, these all *look* strongly linked. But the moment you look **within a single country, over time**, the link is essentially **zero**. Health spending and leisure spending track **GDP and income** — not the longevity quantity itself. *So we report these strictly as descriptions, never as cause and effect* — and we're upfront that with this little data, the right phrase is "we cannot detect an effect," not "there is none." That restraint *is* the result.

---

## 6 · Implications & shocks  *(2 min · total 15:00)*
*On screen: click **6 · Implications & shocks**.*

So — what does it all mean, and what could move it? Only **three families of policy** can shift the balance, and each maps to a term in our identity. **Increase supply** — participation, retention of healthy older workers, and migration. **Ease demand** — automation and productivity, so each job needs fewer human hours. Or **maintain balance** — education, training, and retirement-age policy that keeps skills aligned with need. The East's surplus and the West's shortage are the map for where each lever actually bites.

And because the real world is never *only* slow demographics, this page also measures the **shocks** — straight from our own data. The **COVID** vacancy collapse and its sharp V-shaped rebound — Poland's vacancies fell over forty percent, then snapped back. The **Ukraine war** as a live **refugee supply shock**, lifting the workforce in Poland and Czechia. And **AI** as the slow, structural force reshaping demand, with adoption running all the way from nearly **thirty percent of firms in Norway** down to about **five percent in Romania** — the same West-leads, East-lags gradient we see everywhere in this study.

*That's the answer, the caveats, and the stakes.* I'll hand over now — my colleague will show you why you can believe any of it.

# 🔵 PRESENTER B — "Why believe it"  *(stops 7–10 · ~13½ min)*

## 7 · Methodology — how it works  *(3 min · total 18:00)*
*On screen: click **7 · Methodology**; point at the engine diagram.*

Thank you. A forecast you can't explain is a forecast you can't trust — so let me open the hood, because the credibility of everything you just heard lives here.

For every series — life expectancy, healthy years, employment, and so on — we don't bet on a single model. We **average five simple ones**: a **linear** trend, **damped Holt** smoothing, **drift**, **naïve**, and a mean-reverting **AR-one**. That's the entire engine — and the simplicity is deliberate. Our histories are short, roughly thirteen to twenty annual points, and we use **no neural networks**: with this little data, flexible models overfit and mislead. They'd give you a confident, precise, *wrong* answer.

And we didn't just assert that — we **tested** it. A fair challenge is: surely a model dedicated to the nine-year forecast beats one general model? The chart here says no. At **every** horizon, the single ensemble wins — because a dedicated nine-year model can only learn from a median of **three** training pairs and simply starves. We go one level deeper in honesty, too: on these slow, near-random-walk series, the **naïve "last value" is actually the hardest thing to beat**. So the real victory isn't cleverness — it's **restraint that hugs that naïve floor** instead of drifting off it.

Finally, the **uncertainty bands** are not decoration. They combine three things in quadrature: how much the five models **disagree**, the **real out-of-sample error** we measure in backtesting, and **demographic uncertainty** from migration scenarios. That's why our bands are honestly wide — often twelve to forty percent — rather than a comforting, fake plus-or-minus five. Now let me make that tangible.

## 8 · Model playground — drag it live  *(3 min · total 21:00)*
*On screen: click **8 · Model playground**; actually drag the sliders.*

This is the method you can *touch* — two live toys on one page, and I want you to watch the screen, not me.

The first is the **ensemble uncertainty** widget. As I add more simple models and a little sampling noise — watch — a whole *cloud* of forecasts forms, and our band is simply the spread of that cloud. There's no magic and no hidden assumption: the uncertainty you see is literally the **disagreement among honest simple models**, plus the error we measured out of sample.

The second one is my favourite — **flexibility, the bias–variance tradeoff** — and it's running on a *real* Healthy-Life-Years series for Czechia. This slider is model flexibility. Drag it all the way left, to a flat line — too rigid, it **underfits**, it misses the real trend. Now drag it right, toward a wiggly, high-degree curve, and watch the little chart at the bottom. The **training error keeps falling** — the curve hits every point — but the **cross-validated error explodes**, from about **0.7 up to 15**. *That* is overfitting, live: the model memorising noise it will never see again. The cross-validated sweet spot sits at a **modest** flexibility — degree three here — which is exactly why we use simple models. A neural network would land far to the right, deep in that overfit zone. *Everything in this project is one principled choice to stay near that sweet spot* — and now you've seen, with your own eyes, what's on either side of it.

---

## 9 · Trust & critique — on the stand  *(4 min · total 25:00)*
*On screen: click **9 · Trust & critique**.*

Now the part I'm proudest of: we put **our own model on trial**, and this page is both the defence and the prosecution.

**First, the case for it.** We use rolling-origin cross-validation — hold out the last few years, forecast them from history only, and measure the error. The structural drivers do well: life expectancy at **1.2 percent**, employment and working-life under two. The composed outputs are tight too — **supply at 3.6 percent, demand at 2.2**. And we added a **proper scoring rule, CRPS**, which grades the *whole probability band*, not just the point estimate: skill versus a naïve forecast is **positive for every single driver, averaging plus 0.29**. Strikingly, that's true *even for vacancies*, where the point forecast barely ties naïve — because our **bands are genuinely well-calibrated**. In plain terms: the model knows what it doesn't know.

**Then, the prosecution — our own skeptical audit.** Three findings worth your attention. **One:** we put **bootstrap confidence intervals** on every accuracy number. So vacancies aren't "24 percent" full stop — they're 24 with an honest range of **16 to 34**. Real precision, not false precision. **Two — the big one:** the East–West split partly rides on **self-reported health**, which simply isn't comparable across countries. When we strip that out, the West's shortage and the East's surplus **nearly collapse into each other**. So we flag the cross-country *level* split as our **least robust** headline. **Three**, the same lesson in one number: across countries, the longevity-to-spending links look powerful — R-squared around **0.99**; but *within* a country they fall to essentially **zero**. That 0.99 is pure country size and shared trend; the honest signal is about *nil*.

And here's the through-line: **every** time we reached for a fancier tool — a regression tree, gradient boosting, separate per-horizon specialists, country-by-country vacancy curves — we **measured it, and it lost** to the simple choice. The boosted tree actually fell apart out of sample. *We didn't just prefer simple. We proved simple wins.* That's the whole philosophy of this project: state the error, show the failure points, and never over-claim.

## 10 · What-if sandbox — the finale  *(3.5 min · total 28:30)*
*On screen: click **10 · What-if sandbox**; take a scenario from the audience.*

Let me finish where it gets personal — because behind every one of these numbers is a human question: who keeps working, who retires, who needs care, and who fills the concert halls and the adult-education classes?

This is a **live sandbox**. Three dials — **healthy life years**, **retirement age**, and **participation** — and the screen answers instantly, all built on the very same identities you've seen for the last half hour. It even shows you the **occupied jobs and the open listings** moving as you change participation, so it stays grounded in real labour-market quantities.

Give me a scenario — anyone. *[Take one from the audience; otherwise:]* Let's say longevity keeps improving and people stay **healthy two years longer**, and we nudge **participation** up a notch. Watch: supply rises, the shortage shrinks, and the healthy-retirement dividend swings. Now let's push the **retirement age** the other way — and watch carefully *who* that helps and *who* it strains, because the answer isn't the same in Sofia as it is in Stuttgart.

That's the whole point of this project. Not a single prophecy — a **transparent engine you can interrogate**: honest about what it knows, honest about what it doesn't, and reproducible from raw data to this slider. The structural message is robust: Europe's **longevity dividend is real** and already holding supply up — but it sits on top of a deep **East–West divide**, and these three dials are exactly where policy meets that divide.

Thank you — we'd be glad to take your questions. *[~1½ min buffer remains.]*
